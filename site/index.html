Try it on your computer

Download one file, check it's genuine and run it. It works on Mac, Windows and Linux, and keeps working offline. Allow about 15 minutes, most of it for the download. There's also an experimental browser version with no install, which doesn't yet work on every laptop.

  1. Download the model

    One 2.9 GB file, from Mozilla.ai's official Hugging Face page. It's Qwen3.5-2B, an open model, packaged with Mozilla.ai's llamafile.

    Download Qwen3.5-2B-Q8_0.llamafile

  2. Check it's the genuine file

    We'd ask the same of any download. Run the command for your computer, then compare the result with the checksum below. If it doesn't match exactly, delete the file and don't run it.

    Expected checksum (SHA256)e4ac37318fffd222d802373a5e0c2ec7f78aa3af3744f01eec75a3750e130386
    Mac (in Terminal)shasum -a 256 ~/Downloads/Qwen3.5-2B-Q8_0.llamafile
    Windows (in Command Prompt, after step 3's rename)certutil -hashfile Qwen3.5-2B-Q8_0.llamafile.exe SHA256
    About the "Suspicious" label on Hugging Face. 1 of 57 antivirus engines flags this file. Microsoft Defender, Kaspersky, Sophos, McAfee, Malwarebytes and Bitdefender find nothing. Llamafiles are unusual single-file programs that some pattern-based scanners flag. Read the full VirusTotal report.

  3. Run it

    Mac: open Terminal, paste this and press Enter.

    cd ~/Downloads && chmod +x Qwen3.5-2B-Q8_0.llamafile && ./Qwen3.5-2B-Q8_0.llamafile

    If macOS blocks it, go to System Settings, then Privacy & Security, and choose Allow Anyway. Then press the Up arrow in Terminal and Enter to run it again.

    Windows: rename the file so it ends in .exe, then double-click it.

    Then open http://localhost:8080 in your browser. That's the chat, running on your own computer. To stop it, press Ctrl-C in the Terminal window.

  4. Add the Opsec Check instructions

    In the chat's Settings, paste this into the System Message field and save. Then start a new chat.

    Show the system message
    You are Opsec Check, a security assistant for independent builders and community organisers. You run entirely on the user's own device; nothing they paste leaves their computer. The user will paste a message, job offer, DM, email or link they received. Assess it for scams, impersonation and security risks. Common threats in this community include: - Fake job interviews or "take-home tests" that ask you to download or run software - Impersonators posing as known community members, funders, event organisers or platform staff - Links asking you to "connect", "verify" or "sign in" to an account or wallet - Unexpected meeting invites from someone you know or used to know, whose account may have been hacked - Requests before or during a call to download or "update" Zoom, Teams or other meeting software from a link they send - Urgent requests for money, codes, passwords or recovery phrases Reply in this format: RISK: Low / Medium / High RED FLAGS: short bullet points quoting the suspicious parts WHAT TO DO: 2-4 concrete steps (e.g. verify through an official channel you already use, don't download anything, report to community moderators) Be plain and calm. If you are unsure, say so and recommend verifying through a known official channel. Never tell the user something is definitely safe. Reply in the same language as the message the user pasted. Keep the RISK, RED FLAGS and WHAT TO DO headings, translated if needed.

Why we built it

H.E.R. DAO is a global community of over 2,000 independent builders in more than 20 hubs across Africa, Asia, Europe and the Americas. Many of them do social impact work in their own communities.

Our hubs are in Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Singapore, South Korea, Indonesia, India, Argentina, Colombia, Bolivia, Mexico, Venezuela, Chile, Peru, the UK, France, Germany and Spain, plus our first US city chapter in San Francisco.

As the community has grown, so have the attacks aimed at it: fake job interviews that ask builders to install malware, people impersonating funders and organisers, and links that ask you to "verify" an account or wallet. These hit hardest for people working alone, without a security team to ask.

One member got a message from someone they knew, an old acquaintance, asking them to book a call. On the call, several people they didn't know were waiting. They said they couldn't hear properly and sent a link to "download a new version of Zoom". The link was malicious. The contact's account had been taken over.

Cloud AI tools could help, but they'd ask builders to send the very messages they're worried about to a company's servers. So we went small and local instead.

What testers say

How it works, and who's in control

Runs on your device

The model runs on your own computer. There's no server, no account and no tracking. It works offline.

Small on purpose

A 2-billion-parameter model is enough for one focused job: spotting known patterns of deception in a message.

No surprise changes

Every version is a fixed file with a published checksum. Nothing updates automatically, and you can keep any version you trust.

Shaped by the community

The threats it looks for come from what our members report. Before each release, updates will be reviewed by the community.

Open source

Built on Qwen3.5-2B and Mozilla.ai's llamafile, both Apache 2.0. Our instructions and threat patterns are open for anyone to adapt.

Honest about limits

It can be wrong, so it never says a message is definitely safe. When in doubt, it tells you to check through a channel you already trust.
